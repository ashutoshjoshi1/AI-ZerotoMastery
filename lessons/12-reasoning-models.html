Session 1 of 3From prompted chain-of-thought to trained reasoning: test-time compute, how reasoning models are trained, thinking in an API

Thinking is computation#

In lesson 11 you asked a model to work through a problem before answering, and it got more right. The reason is mechanical. A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per token (lesson 08), so a model that answers "1368" straight away has had one forward pass per output token to work it out. A model that first writes "6 hours is 21,600 seconds, divided by 15 is 1,440 readings, 5% fail, so 1,368" has given itself hundreds of extra passes, each able to read the intermediate results of the last. The reasoning text is scratch memory.

Prompted chain-of-thought has two weaknesses. The model was never trained to use that scratch space well, so its chains are often linear, confident and wrong: it rarely checks a step or backtracks. And you decide in advance how much thinking to ask for. A reasoning model fixes both: it is trained so that the thinking is useful (it tries approaches, notices errors and backs up), and it decides how long to think per question, within a budget you set.

Test-time compute: the second scaling axis#

The scaling laws in lesson 09 relate accuracy to parameters and training data: spend more at training time and the model gets better at everything, for a price paid once. Test-time compute is spending more at inference time, per question: longer thinking, several attempts, a verifier. It is paid on every query, but you choose where to spend it: a hard question can get a thousand times more computation than an easy one.

Snell et al. (2024) studied how to allocate a fixed test-time budget and found that the best strategy depends on the question: easier questions gain most from letting the model revise its answer, harder ones from sampling many attempts and searching among them. "More thinking" is a family of techniques, not one knob; session 2 builds them from scratch.

How reasoning models are trained#

The recipe has three ingredients. Lessons 19 and 25 cover the mechanics; here is the shape.

  1. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. Take problems a program can check: maths with a known answer, code with unit tests. Let the model write a chain of thought and an answer, score the answer 1 or 0, and update the model to make the chains that led to correct answers more likely. Nobody writes example reasoning; the model discovers what helps. The DeepSeek-R1 report (2025) showed that this stage alone, applied to a base model, produces long chains with self-checking and backtracking, and that chains grow longer during training because longer thinking earns more reward.
  2. Supervised polishing. Pure RL produces chains that work but read badly. Supervised fine-tuning on curated reasoning traces, then the usual preference tuning, makes the output usable.
  3. Distillation. Generate (problem, chain of thought, answer) examples with the big model and fine-tune smaller models on them. They pick up the reasoning style and much of the accuracy at a fraction of the cost, which is why reasoning models exist at every size.

Two consequences for engineers. The reward is only about the answer being marked correct, so the model learns whatever gets it marked correct, sometimes including flaws in the checker (session 3 returns to this). And because the training problems are maths, code and puzzles, that is where the gains are largest.

Thinking in an API#

Providers expose thinking as extra content in the response. With the Anthropic API, current models think by default and an effort level controls how much; the previous generation used an explicit thinking budget of tokens, as other providers still do. Either way the response holds thinking blocks before the text blocks. As in lesson 10, client and MODEL come from the notebook's setup cell, along with MODEL_FAST for a cheap model.

Python
question = ("A sensor logs one reading every 15 seconds and 5% of readings fail a "
            "quality check. How many good readings does it keep in 6 hours?")

resp = client.messages.create(
    model=MODEL,
    max_tokens=8000,                                         # thinking counts against this cap
    thinking={"type": "adaptive", "display": "summarized"},  # return a readable summary of the thinking
    output_config={"effort": "medium"},                      # low | medium | high | xhigh | max
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": question}],
)
for block in resp.content:                                   # read blocks by type, never by position
    if block.type == "thinking":
        print("THINKING:", block.thinking[:400], "...\n")
    elif block.type == "text":
        print("ANSWER:", block.text)
print(resp.stop_reason, "| input", resp.usage.input_tokens,
      "| output incl. thinking", resp.usage.output_tokens)

Three things to notice. The thinking comes back as a summary if you ask for one and as an empty block if you do not, and you are billed for all of it either way. The answer is no longer resp.content[0]: read blocks by type, never by position. And max_tokens covers thinking plus answer, so a cap sized for a one-word reply can be used up before the model says anything; the response then ends with stop_reason == "max_tokens" and no text.

Thinking controls change with every model generation

At the time of writing, current Claude models take thinking={"type": "adaptive"} and output_config={"effort": ...}, and reject the older budget_tokens setting and non-default temperatures; other providers have settings of their own. Keep the model name and its thinking settings together in one place and read the current documentation when you change models: this is the part of your code most likely to break on an upgrade.

Watching it think#

Thinking adds seconds before the first visible token. Stream, and show progress instead of a blank screen:

Python
with client.messages.stream(
    model=MODEL, max_tokens=8000,
    thinking={"type": "adaptive", "display": "summarized"},
    output_config={"effort": "medium"},
    messages=[{"role": "user", "content": question}],
) as stream:
    for event in stream:
        if event.type == "content_block_start":
            print(f"\n[{event.content_block.type}]")
        elif event.type == "content_block_delta":
            if event.delta.type == "thinking_delta":
                print(event.delta.thinking, end="", flush=True)
            elif event.delta.type == "text_delta":
                print(event.delta.text, end="", flush=True)
    final = stream.get_final_message()
print("\n", final.usage.output_tokens, "output tokens")

Thinking also interleaves with tool use: a model with tools can think, call a tool, read the result and think again within one turn. When you send tool results back, include the thinking blocks exactly as you received them so the model continues the same line of reasoning. Session 3 has the example.

Effort, cost and latency#

Thinking tokens are output tokens, the expensive kind, all generated before the answer starts. Effort is the dial. Wrap the call in a helper that records usage and time, then sweep it:

Python
import time

PRICE_IN, PRICE_OUT = 2.0, 10.0     # USD per million tokens at the time of writing: check the current price list

def ask(question, effort="medium", max_tokens=8000, model=None):
    """One call with adaptive thinking. Returns (visible text, usage, seconds)."""
    t0 = time.time()
    resp = client.messages.create(
        model=model or MODEL, max_tokens=max_tokens,
        thinking={"type": "adaptive"},
        output_config={"effort": effort},
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": question}],
    )
    text = "".join(b.text for b in resp.content if b.type == "text")
    if resp.stop_reason == "max_tokens":
        text += "\n[cut off: the budget went on thinking; raise max_tokens or lower effort]"
    return text, resp.usage, time.time() - t0

def cost(usage):
    return usage.input_tokens / 1e6 * PRICE_IN + usage.output_tokens / 1e6 * PRICE_OUT

for effort in ["low", "medium", "high"]:
    text, usage, secs = ask(question, effort=effort)
    last_line = text.strip().splitlines()[-1][:60] if text.strip() else "(no text)"
    print(f"{effort:>6}: {usage.output_tokens:>5} output tokens, {secs:4.1f}s, "
          f"${cost(usage):.4f}  ->  {last_line}")

Output tokens and latency climb with effort, and on an easy question the final line is the same at every level. That is the economics of reasoning models in one line: you pay for thinking whether or not the question needed it, so choose the effort per request, not once for the whole application.

When reasoning helps, and when it does not#

Task Does thinking help? Why
Multi-step arithmetic, unit conversions, dates Yes, a lot Intermediate values must be written down to be used
Writing and debugging code Yes It can trace execution and test hypotheses first
Planning under many constraints Yes Constraints interact; a first guess rarely satisfies all of them
Analysis of a long document against several criteria Often The model can gather the evidence before judging
Factual lookup No The fact is in the weights or it is not; thinking cannot create knowledge
Classification, extraction, formatting Rarely Pattern matching; a better prompt does more than a longer think
Latency-critical paths (autocomplete, voice) No Seconds of silence break the experience; route hard cases to a slower path

Hands-on: when does thinking pay?

At your computer 12-reasoning-models.ipynb, cell “Hands-on: when does thinking pay?”

  1. Write six questions with checkable answers: three one-step lookups and three multi-step word problems. Ask for the answer in a fixed format so a small grade() function can mark it.
  2. Run each question at effort low and high with ask(), recording whether it was right, output tokens and seconds.
  3. For each (class, effort) pair compute cost per correct answer.
  4. You are done when you can state, with numbers, which class should run at which effort.
Show a solution
Python
import re

easy = [("What is the capital of Peru? Reply with the city name only.", "lima"),
        ("How many minutes are there in a day? Reply with the number only.", "1440"),
        ("What is the chemical symbol for sodium? Reply with the symbol only.", "na")]
hard = [("A pump moves 18 litres a minute for 2.5 hours, then 12 litres a minute for 45 minutes. "
         "How many litres in total? End with 'Answer: <number>'.", "3240"),
        ("A train leaves at 09:40 and arrives at 14:05 the same day. It stops for a total of 35 minutes. "
         "For how many minutes is it moving? End with 'Answer: <number>'.", "230"),
        ("A 1.2 kW heater runs 4 hours a night for 30 nights. Electricity costs 0.25 per kWh. "
         "What is the total cost? End with 'Answer: <number>'.", "36")]

def grade(text, gold):
    tokens = re.findall(r"[a-z]+|-?\d[\d,]*\.?\d*", text.lower())
    tokens = [t.replace(",", "").removesuffix(".0") for t in tokens]
    return gold in tokens

for label, qs in [("easy", easy), ("hard", hard)]:
    for effort in ["low", "high"]:
        right, tokens, dollars, secs = 0, 0, 0.0, 0.0
        for q, gold in qs:
            text, usage, s = ask(q, effort=effort)
            right += grade(text, gold); tokens += usage.output_tokens
            dollars += cost(usage); secs += s
        per_correct = dollars / right if right else float("inf")
        print(f"{label} / {effort:>4}: {right}/{len(qs)} correct, {tokens:>5} output tokens, "
              f"{secs:4.1f}s, ${dollars:.4f} total, ${per_correct:.4f} per correct answer")

What to observe: on the lookups both effort levels are usually 3/3 and the low-effort rows use far fewer tokens and seconds, so cost per correct answer is several times lower. On the word problems the result can go either way with a current model, but tokens and time always grow with effort. If every row is 3/3, make the hard questions harder until a difference appears; that boundary is the information you want.

Session 2 of 3Test-time compute you can build: self-consistency, best-of-N, verifiers and search, in simulation and with a real model

Test-time compute you can build yourself#

A reasoning model spends its compute inside one long answer. You can also spend compute around a model, with any model, by treating it as a noisy solver: sample it several times and choose well among the samples. The techniques differ in how they choose.

  • Self-consistency: sample N answers, return the most common one (majority vote).
  • Best-of-N: sample N answers, score each with a verifier, return the top scorer.
  • Sequential revision: ask the model to critique and improve its own answer, repeatedly.
  • Search over steps: build the solution step by step, scoring partial solutions and keeping the best few (beam search) or exploring a tree of alternatives (lesson 24).

Before paying for API calls, simulate: a simulation shows the regimes in a minute, and the regimes are what matter.

Self-consistency: sample many, vote#

Wang et al. (2022) observed that a model's correct answers agree with each other, while its mistakes are spread over many different wrong answers. Vote, and the agreement wins. The condition is exact: let ppp be the probability that one sample is correct and qqq the probability of the single most common wrong answer. As NNN grows, majority voting drives accuracy towards 1 if p>qp > qp>q and towards 0 if p<qp < qp<q. ppp need not exceed one half; it only has to beat each wrong answer individually.

Python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def vote_accuracy(p, wrong, n, trials=20000):
    """Majority vote over n independent samples. Index 0 is the correct answer, drawn
    with probability p; the wrong answers 1..k are drawn with the probabilities in `wrong`."""
    probs = np.array([p, *wrong])
    answers = rng.choice(len(probs), size=(trials, n), p=probs)
    counts = (answers[:, :, None] == np.arange(len(probs))).sum(axis=1)   # votes per answer
    counts = counts + rng.random(counts.shape) * 0.5                      # break ties at random
    return np.mean(counts.argmax(axis=1) == 0)

spread = [0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 0.1]      # p = 0.6: wrong answers spread thinly over four distractors
close = [0.3, 0.2, 0.15]          # p = 0.35: the most common wrong answer is nearly as likely
lumped = [0.5, 0.1, 0.05]         # p = 0.35: one wrong answer is MORE likely than the right one
for n in [1, 3, 5, 9, 15, 25, 51]:
    print(f"N={n:>2}   spread {vote_accuracy(0.6, spread, n):.3f}   "
          f"close {vote_accuracy(0.35, close, n):.3f}   lumped {vote_accuracy(0.35, lumped, n):.3f}")
Output
N= 1   spread 0.597   close 0.354   lumped 0.346
N= 3   spread 0.718   close 0.379   lumped 0.337
N= 5   spread 0.838   close 0.413   lumped 0.338
N= 9   spread 0.939   close 0.463   lumped 0.309
N=15   spread 0.986   close 0.510   lumped 0.263
N=25   spread 0.999   close 0.566   lumped 0.206
N=51   spread 1.000   close 0.652   lumped 0.119

Three regimes. With the errors spread (p=0.6p = 0.6p=0.6, no distractor above 0.1) voting is almost free accuracy: five samples take the solver from 60% to 84%. In a close race (p=0.35p = 0.35p=0.35 against a distractor at 0.3) voting helps slowly, and the first few samples barely move it. With a lumped error (a systematic mistake made half the time) voting hurts, and the more you sample the more reliably you get the wrong answer. Self-consistency cancels random errors and cements systematic ones.

A common mistake is to apply it to open-ended outputs. Voting needs answers that can be compared for equality: a number, a label, a canonical string. For prose there is nothing to vote on; you need a verifier.

Best-of-N with a verifier#

If you can score answers, they need not agree: generate N and keep the best score. The verifier can be a program (unit tests, a type checker, a constraint check), a second model prompted as a judge, or a trained reward model, and its quality decides everything. Model it as a scorer that separates correct from wrong answers by a margin ddd, with a small fraction of wrong answers that fool it completely:

Python
def best_of_n_accuracy(p, n, d, blind=0.0, trials=20000):
    """Sample n answers, let a verifier score each one and keep the top score.
    Correct answers score N(d, 1) and wrong ones N(0, 1), so d is how well the
    verifier separates them. A fraction `blind` of wrong answers fool it completely
    and score N(d + 3, 1)."""
    correct = rng.random((trials, n)) < p
    fooled = ~correct & (rng.random((trials, n)) < blind)
    scores = rng.normal(0, 1, size=(trials, n)) + d * correct + (d + 3) * fooled
    picked = scores.argmax(axis=1)
    return np.mean(correct[np.arange(trials), picked])

for n in [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64]:
    print(f"N={n:>2}   weak verifier {best_of_n_accuracy(0.3, n, 1.0):.3f}   "
          f"strong {best_of_n_accuracy(0.3, n, 3.0):.3f}   "
          f"strong with blind spots {best_of_n_accuracy(0.3, n, 3.0, blind=0.03):.3f}")
Output
N= 1   weak verifier 0.304   strong 0.303   strong with blind spots 0.301
N= 2   weak verifier 0.411   strong 0.502   strong with blind spots 0.497
N= 4   weak verifier 0.524   strong 0.738   strong with blind spots 0.691
N= 8   weak verifier 0.615   strong 0.921   strong with blind spots 0.784
N=16   weak verifier 0.705   strong 0.988   strong with blind spots 0.720
N=32   weak verifier 0.764   strong 0.998   strong with blind spots 0.543
N=64   weak verifier 0.819   strong 1.000   strong with blind spots 0.325

A strong verifier turns a 30% solver into a near-perfect one by N=16N = 16N=16; a weak one helps slowly. The third column is the one to remember. With blind spots (here 3% of wrong answers score above every correct one) accuracy rises until about N=8N = 8N=8 and then falls: the more candidates you generate, the more likely one lands in a blind spot, and the selection step then prefers it. Sampling harder against an imperfect judge finds the judge's mistakes. This is Goodhart's law in miniature, and the same mechanism as reward hacking in training.

So measure the verifier before you build best-of-N: its precision bounds the accuracy you can reach, and its false-positive rate sets the NNN beyond which more sampling makes things worse.

Outcome versus process: verify the steps#

A verifier that sees only the final answer is an outcome reward model (ORM); a process reward model (PRM) scores every step. Lightman et al. (2023) trained a PRM on step-level human labels for maths solutions and found it selected correct solutions more reliably than an ORM. The reason is combinatorial. If a solution has sss steps and the solver gets each right with probability qqq, sampling whole solutions and checking the outcome succeeds with probability 1−(1−qs)N1 - (1 - q^s)^N1−(1−qs)N, while checking every step and resampling only that step (a perfect PRM, kkk candidates per step) succeeds with (1−(1−q)k)s(1 - (1 - q)^k)^s(1−(1−q)k)s:

Python
def outcome_search(q, steps, n):
    """Sample n complete solutions; a perfect outcome verifier keeps a correct one if any exists."""
    return 1 - (1 - q ** steps) ** n

def process_search(q, steps, k):
    """At every step sample k candidates; a perfect process verifier keeps a correct one if any exists."""
    return (1 - (1 - q) ** k) ** steps

for budget in [1, 2, 4, 8]:
    print(f"{budget} candidate(s) per step:  outcome search {outcome_search(0.8, 6, budget):.3f}   "
          f"process search {process_search(0.8, 6, budget):.3f}")
Output
1 candidate(s) per step:  outcome search 0.262   process search 0.262
2 candidate(s) per step:  outcome search 0.456   process search 0.783
4 candidate(s) per step:  outcome search 0.704   process search 0.990
8 candidate(s) per step:  outcome search 0.912   process search 1.000

With six steps at 80% each, a whole solution is right only 26% of the time. Eight whole samples get you to 91%; eight candidates per step get you to effectively 100% for the same number of generated steps. Step-level feedback is dense feedback. The price is the verifier: step labels are expensive, a learned PRM is noisy, and the search code is more complex. Beam search generalises the idea (keep the best bbb partial solutions at each step), and Monte Carlo tree search adds backtracking: lesson 24 implements MCTS from scratch and shows how AlphaZero combines search with learned policy and value networks, the same search-plus-verifier idea applied to games.

Sequential revision ("find and fix any mistakes in your answer") is the cheapest technique to implement and the least reliable without a real check: models are poor at spotting their own errors from the text alone and often "fix" a correct answer into a wrong one. Give the critic something concrete, such as a test result or a constraint violation. With that, revision loops are the backbone of coding agents (lesson 15).

The same experiment with a real model#

Now with real calls. The problem set is ten hand-written arithmetic word problems with integer answers, plus a parser that pulls the final number out of a reply:

Python
import re

PROBLEMS = [
    ("A sensor logs one reading every 15 seconds. How many readings does it log in 6 hours?", 1440),
    ("A battery stores 2400 Wh. An instrument draws 30 W continuously. For how many hours can it run before the battery is empty?", 80),
    ("A campaign budget is 5000. Each site visit costs 350 and each data upload costs 12. The team made 9 visits and 60 uploads. How much budget is left?", 1130),
    ("Three air samplers each run for 4 hours at 1.5 litres per minute. How many litres of air do they sample in total?", 1080),
    ("A dataset has 1250 rows. 8% of them fail a quality check and are removed. The remaining rows are split 80/20 into training and test sets. How many rows are in the test set?", 230),
    ("A spectrometer takes a 12-second exposure followed by a 3-second readout, then starts the next exposure. How many complete exposures fit in 20 minutes?", 80),
    ("A shipment of 7 crates weighs 2.1 tonnes in total. Each empty crate weighs 40 kg. What is the mass of the contents in kg?", 1820),
    ("Node A sends 48 packets a minute and node B sends 36. Each packet is 250 bytes. How many kilobytes (1 kB = 1000 bytes) do they send together in one hour?", 1260),
    ("A station has 4 instruments that each produce 180 MB a day. Its link uploads 25 MB a minute and is available for 2 hours a day. How many MB remain un-uploaded at the end of 5 days?", 0),
    ("A technician drives to site A, 42 km away, then 18 km further to site B, then 25 km further to site C, and drives straight home along the same road. Fuel costs 0.2 per km. What is the total fuel cost?", 34),
]
SUFFIX = "\nEnd your reply with a line of the form 'Answer: <number>'."

def extract_number(text: str):
    """Pull the final numeric answer out of a reply: the 'Answer:' line if present, else the last number."""
    m = re.search(r"Answer:\s*\$?\s*(-?[\d,]*\.?\d+)", text)
    found = m.group(1) if m else (re.findall(r"-?[\d,]*\.?\d+", text) or [None])[-1]
    if found is None:
        return None
    value = float(found.replace(",", ""))
    return int(value) if value == int(value) else value

print(len(PROBLEMS), "problems")
print(extract_number("Total is 2,100 - 280 = 1,820 kg.\nAnswer: 1,820"), extract_number("about 34.0"), extract_number("no digits here"))
Output
10 problems
1820 34 None

Three conditions: a plain answer at low effort, self-consistency with five low-effort samples, and one high-effort answer. The self-consistency paper sampled at a moderate temperature; current Claude models reject non-default temperatures, which is fine, because default sampling is already random and repeated calls give different chains.

Python
from collections import Counter

def plain(q):                                  # the fast path: low effort, answer only
    text, usage, secs = ask(q + SUFFIX, effort="low", max_tokens=2000)
    return extract_number(text), [usage], secs

def self_consistency(q, n=5):                  # sample n times at low effort, majority vote
    votes, usages, secs = [], [], 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        text, usage, s = ask(q + SUFFIX, effort="low", max_tokens=2000)
        votes.append(extract_number(text)); usages.append(usage); secs += s
    return Counter(votes).most_common(1)[0][0], usages, secs

def with_thinking(q):                          # one sample, high effort
    text, usage, secs = ask(q + SUFFIX, effort="high", max_tokens=8000)
    return extract_number(text), [usage], secs

def evaluate(method, **kw):
    right, tokens, dollars, seconds = 0, 0, 0.0, 0.0
    for q, gold in PROBLEMS:
        pred, usages, secs = method(q, **kw)
        right += (pred == gold); seconds += secs
        tokens += sum(u.output_tokens for u in usages); dollars += sum(cost(u) for u in usages)
    label = f"{method.__name__} {kw if kw else ''}".strip()
    print(f"{label:>24}: {right}/{len(PROBLEMS)} correct, {tokens:>6} output tokens, "
          f"${dollars:.4f}, {seconds:.0f}s")

evaluate(plain)
evaluate(self_consistency, n=5)
evaluate(with_thinking)

Do not expect drama. These problems are easy for a current model, so the plain path may already get most of them, and the expensive conditions will cost several times more for a point or two. You will see something like "plain 8/10, voting 9/10 at five times the tokens, thinking 10/10 at two or three times the tokens", but your numbers will differ, and with ten problems a difference of one is noise. Make the problems harder and the gap opens. Keep your numbers; session 3 reuses them.

Prompting a model that thinks#

Prompts written for chat models are often too prescriptive for reasoning models. If you spell out "first do A, then B, then C", a model that would have planned a better route follows your steps instead, and its thinking goes on obeying you. Give it the goal, the constraints and what a good answer looks like, then let it plan.

Micromanaged
Step 1: list every instrument and its last calibration date.
Step 2: for each instrument, add 90 days to get the due date.
Step 3: sort the due dates.
Step 4: assign the earliest three to next week, Monday to Wednesday.
Step 5: write the schedule as a table.
Goal, constraints, success criteria
Produce next week's calibration schedule for the instruments listed below.

Constraints:
- An instrument is due 90 days after its last calibration; overdue ones come first.
- One technician, at most two calibrations a day, none on Friday.
- Sites A and C cannot be visited on the same day (4 hours apart).

A good schedule has no overdue instrument left unscheduled if capacity allows,
explains any that had to wait, and is a table with columns day, site, instrument.
If the data are inconsistent (a future calibration date), say so instead of guessing.

Also: skip "think step by step" (the model already does, and some providers decline requests to reproduce hidden reasoning verbatim); put all the inputs in, since the model reasons about what you gave it and cannot ask for more; and specify the final format so your code can skip the explanation. Few-shot examples still help with format, less with reasoning.

Give it a way to check its work#

Thinking is reasoning about the model's own beliefs; a tool turns a belief into a fact. The most effective thing you can do for a reasoning model on a checkable task is to hand it the checker: a calculator, a test runner, a schema validator, the real database. Here the model gets a safe arithmetic tool and, with thinking on, thinks, calls it, reads the result and continues (the full tool loop is lesson 15):

Python
import ast, operator

OPS = {ast.Add: operator.add, ast.Sub: operator.sub, ast.Mult: operator.mul,
       ast.Div: operator.truediv, ast.USub: operator.neg}

def calculate(expression: str) -> str:
    """Evaluate an arithmetic expression safely: numbers and + - * / only, no names, no calls."""
    def ev(node):
        if isinstance(node, ast.Constant) and isinstance(node.value, (int, float)):
            return node.value
        if isinstance(node, ast.BinOp) and type(node.op) in OPS:
            return OPS[type(node.op)](ev(node.left), ev(node.right))
        if isinstance(node, ast.UnaryOp) and type(node.op) in OPS:
            return OPS[type(node.op)](ev(node.operand))
        raise ValueError("unsupported expression")
    try:
        return str(ev(ast.parse(expression, mode="eval").body))
    except Exception as e:
        return f"error: {e}"

tools = [{"name": "calculate",
          "description": "Evaluate an arithmetic expression exactly. Use it to check every calculation before you answer.",
          "input_schema": {"type": "object", "properties": {"expression": {"type": "string"}},
                           "required": ["expression"]}}]

messages = [{"role": "user", "content": PROBLEMS[9][0] + SUFFIX}]
while True:
    resp = client.messages.create(model=MODEL, max_tokens=8000, thinking={"type": "adaptive"},
                                  output_config={"effort": "medium"}, tools=tools, messages=messages)
    messages.append({"role": "assistant", "content": resp.content})   # thinking blocks go back unchanged
    if resp.stop_reason != "tool_use":
        break
    results = []
    for b in resp.content:
        if b.type == "tool_use":
            value = calculate(b.input["expression"])
            print("checked:", b.input["expression"], "=", value)
            results.append({"type": "tool_result", "tool_use_id": b.id, "content": value})
    messages.append({"role": "user", "content": results})
print("".join(b.text for b in resp.content if b.type == "text"))
print("gold:", PROBLEMS[9][1])

Note the line that appends resp.content back unchanged: the thinking blocks travel with the tool calls, so the model resumes its reasoning rather than starting over.

The visible reasoning is not the cause#

It is tempting to read the thinking as an explanation. Be careful. The chain of thought is text the model generated, conditioned on the question; the answer is text generated after it, and nothing forces the second to be caused by the first. Turpin et al. (2023) showed that a model's stated reasoning can leave out the factor that actually changed its answer, such as a bias planted in the prompt, and later work on reasoning models found the same with hints: the model uses the hint and does not mention it. Providers also return summaries, and RL rewards answers rather than honest narration. Reasoning faithfulness is not guaranteed.

So use the visible reasoning for debugging and intuition, never as the sole basis for a safety or compliance decision ("the model said it checked the policy, so it did"). Verify outputs with independent tools and evaluations (lesson 17), treat reasoning monitoring as one signal among several (lesson 22), and see lesson 30 for what it takes to learn what a model actually computed.

Evaluating reasoning systems#

pass@k and pass^k#

Because reasoning systems sample, one accuracy number hides what you need. pass@k is the probability that at least one of kkk attempts is correct: the right metric when a reliable verifier can pick the good attempt, as with code and tests. pass^k is the probability that all kkk attempts are correct: what a user who runs your agent on the same task every day experiences, and the one that measures reliability. From nnn samples per problem of which ccc are correct, the unbiased estimator of Chen et al. (2021) and the plug-in estimate are

pass@k=1−(n−ck)(nk),passk≈(cn)k.\text{pass@}k = 1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}, \qquad \text{pass}^k \approx \left(\frac{c}{n}\right)^k .pass@k=1−(kn​)(kn−c​)​,passk≈(nc​)k.
Python
from math import comb

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    """Unbiased estimate of pass@k from n samples of which c were correct (Chen et al. 2021)."""
    if n - c < k:
        return 1.0
    return 1.0 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
per_problem_p = rng.beta(2, 2, size=40)            # 40 problems of varying difficulty
n = 20
correct_counts = rng.binomial(n, per_problem_p)    # n samples per problem
p_hat = correct_counts / n
for k in [1, 2, 5, 10]:
    pk = np.mean([pass_at_k(n, c, k) for c in correct_counts])
    ppk = np.mean(p_hat ** k)                      # pass^k: all k attempts succeed
    print(f"k={k:>2}   pass@k {pk:.3f}   pass^k {ppk:.3f}")

Same system, same samples: pass@10 is 97% and pass^10 is 7%. A report that quotes pass@k without saying so describes a best case with a free oracle. For a product, ask which of the two you are selling.

def pareto(points):
    """Keep the (name, cost, accuracy) points that no cheaper point matches or beats."""
    kept, best = [], -1.0
    for name, cost, acc in sorted(points, key=lambda t: t[1]):
        if acc > best:
            kept.append((name, cost, acc)); best = acc
    return kept

Routing#

Most traffic does not need reasoning. A router sends each request to the cheapest model likely to handle it and escalates the rest. The simplest router is a rule:

Python
HARD_SIGNS = [
    r"(\d[\d.,]*\D+){3,}\d",                             # three or more numbers to combine
    r"\b(why|plan|schedule|optimi[sz]e|prove|debug|trade-?off)\b",
    r"\b(constraints?|subject to|at most|at least|must not)\b",
    r"```",                                              # a code block
]

def route(question: str) -> str:
    """Return 'fast' or 'reasoning'. It errs towards 'reasoning': a wrong 'fast'
    costs accuracy, a wrong 'reasoning' only costs money."""
    hard = any(re.search(p, question, flags=re.I | re.S) for p in HARD_SIGNS)
    return "reasoning" if hard or len(question.split()) > 60 else "fast"

questions = ["What is the capital of Peru?",
             "Translate 'good morning' into French.",
             PROBLEMS[9][0],
             "Plan a 3-day calibration schedule for 5 sites subject to the constraints below: ..."]
for q in questions:
    print(f"{route(q):>9}: {q[:72]}")
Output
     fast: What is the capital of Peru?
     fast: Translate 'good morning' into French.
reasoning: A technician drives to site A, 42 km away, then 18 km further to site B,
reasoning: Plan a 3-day calibration schedule for 5 sites subject to the constraints

Better routers use a signal rather than a rule: a cheap model asked to rate the difficulty, the fast model's own confidence from the structured output above (escalate below a threshold), or disagreement: take two cheap samples, return the answer if they agree, escalate if they do not. Agreement among cheap samples is evidence that the question was easy. Whichever you choose, evaluate the router as a system: accuracy and cost per question against always-fast and always-reasoning baselines, on your own traffic.

Failure modes#

Symptom What is happening What to do
Trivial questions take seconds and thousands of tokens Overthinking: every input is treated as hard Lower the effort on that route; send easy cases to a fast model
Long, plausible reasoning ending in a wrong answer A confident chain rationalising an early mistake Give it a tool or a test; vote or verify; never read length as quality
Thinking block but no answer, stop_reason is max_tokens Budget exhaustion Raise max_tokens, lower effort, or split the question
"All tests pass", but the code special-cases the test inputs Reward hacking learnt from training against checkers Hold out tests the model never sees; review diffs; forbid special-casing in the prompt and the grader
Accuracy gets worse with more samples Voting on a systematic error, or best-of-N finding the verifier's blind spots Measure ppp and qqq; measure verifier precision; cap NNN
The reasoning says it checked something it did not Unfaithful reasoning Verify with tools; never gate a safety decision on the narration

Python
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
M = 3000
p_fast = rng.beta(4, 2, size=M)                     # per-question accuracy of the fast model
p_reason = np.clip(p_fast + 0.3, 0, 0.98)           # the reasoning model is better, most where fast is weak
C_FAST, C_REASON, K = 1.0, 15.0, 3                  # relative costs; wrong answers spread over K distractors

def sample(p):
    """A sample from a model with accuracy p: 0 is the right answer, 1..K are wrong ones."""
    return 0 if rng.random() < p else rng.integers(1, K + 1)

def run(policy):
    right, cost, escalated = 0, 0.0, 0
    for pf, pr in zip(p_fast, p_reason):
        if policy == "fast":
            right += sample(pf) == 0; cost += C_FAST
        elif policy == "reasoning":
            right += sample(pr) == 0; cost += C_REASON
        else:                                          # two fast samples; escalate only if they disagree
            a, b = sample(pf), sample(pf); cost += 2 * C_FAST
            if a == b:
                right += a == 0
            else:
                right += sample(pr) == 0; cost += C_REASON; escalated += 1
    print(f"{policy:>12}: accuracy {right / M:.3f}   cost per question {cost / M:5.2f}   escalated {escalated / M:.0%}")

for policy in ["fast", "reasoning", "disagreement"]:
    run(policy)

Check yourself#

1. Why does writing out intermediate steps improve a transformer's accuracy on multi-step problems?

Computation per token is fixed, so the only way to compute more is to generate more tokens. The reasoning text is working memory.

2. A model answers a question correctly 35% of the time and gives one particular wrong answer 50% of the time. What happens to accuracy if you sample 25 times and take the majority answer?

Majority voting converges on whichever single answer is most probable. Voting helps only when the correct answer beats every individual wrong answer.

3. Best-of-N with a learned verifier improves accuracy up to N = 8 and then gets worse. What is the most likely explanation?

Optimising against an imperfect judge eventually finds its blind spots: Goodhart's law applied to selection.

4. Your agent will run the same nightly task every day and must not fail. Which metric should you report?

pass@k assumes a free oracle picks the good attempt. A user who gets a different result on a bad day experiences pass^k.

5. A model's visible thinking says "I checked this request against the data-handling policy and it complies." Can your compliance pipeline rely on that?

Reasoning faithfulness is not guaranteed, and summaries hide detail. Use the narration for debugging, not as evidence.

Key takeaways

  • Reasoning is computation: more generated tokens means more passes through the network. Reasoning models are trained with RL on checkable problems to use that scratch space well; test-time compute is a second scaling axis you control per request.
  • In the API, thinking arrives as blocks you read by type, costs output tokens and latency, and is controlled by an effort level or budget that changes between model generations.
  • Self-consistency helps when the correct answer beats every individual wrong answer and hurts otherwise; best-of-N is bounded by the verifier and can get worse with more samples; step-level verification is far more sample-efficient than outcome checking.
  • Prompt with goals, constraints and success criteria, give the model tools to check its work, and never treat the visible reasoning as the true cause of the answer.
  • Evaluate with pass@k and pass^k, accuracy per dollar and per second, and budget sweeps; route easy traffic to a fast model and escalate the rest.
