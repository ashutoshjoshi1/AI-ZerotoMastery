07 Text as numbers, sequences and embeddings

How text becomes vectors whose geometry captures meaning, how recurrent networks read sequences one step at a time, and the bottleneck that motivated attention.

Study plan: 2 sessions of about an hour

Tick a session when you finish it. Progress is saved in this browser only.

Code for this lesson: notebooks/07-sequences-embeddings.ipynb, with worked solutions in notebooks/solutions/.

The problem: models need numbers#

Neural networks multiply matrices, so text has to become numbers. How you do that decides what the model can learn. This lesson follows the historical path from naive encodings to learned embeddings and recurrent networks, because each step explains a design choice in the transformer you will build next.

One-hot vectors and their limits#

Give every word in the vocabulary an index, and represent a word as a vector of zeros with a single 1 at its index. With a 50,000-word vocabulary, each word is a 50,000-dimensional vector.

Two problems. The vectors are huge and almost entirely zeros. Worse, every pair of words is equally different: the dot product of "cat" and "kitten" is 0, the same as "cat" and "carburettor". The representation knows nothing about meaning.

The distributional hypothesis#

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps." (J. R. Firth, 1957)

Words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. "Cat" and "dog" both show up near "sat", "ate", "the", "chased"; "Paris" and "Berlin" both show up near "capital". If we describe each word by the contexts it appears in, similar words get similar descriptions automatically.

Embeddings from counting, in twenty lines#

Before neural networks, you could build word vectors by counting co-occurrences and compressing the counts with the SVD from lesson 02. Doing it yourself makes the idea concrete.

import numpy as np

corpus = """the cat sat on the mat . the dog sat on the rug . a cat chased a mouse .
a dog chased a cat . the mouse ate the cheese . the cat ate the fish .
the dog ate the bone . a kitten is a young cat . a puppy is a young dog .
the kitten sat on the rug . the puppy sat on the mat . the mouse ran from the cat .
the dog ran in the park . the cat slept on the mat . the dog slept on the rug .
paris is the capital of france . berlin is the capital of germany . rome is the capital of italy .
france exports wine . germany exports cars . italy exports wine .""".split()

vocab = sorted(set(corpus))
idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
V = len(vocab)

# 1. count how often each word appears within 2 positions of each other word
C = np.zeros((V, V))
for i, w in enumerate(corpus):
    for j in range(max(0, i - 2), min(len(corpus), i + 3)):
        if i != j:
            C[idx[w], idx[corpus[j]]] += 1

# 2. positive pointwise mutual information: how much more often than chance do they co-occur?
total = C.sum()
pw = C.sum(1, keepdims=True) / total
pc = C.sum(0, keepdims=True) / total
with np.errstate(divide="ignore"):
    ppmi = np.maximum(np.log((C / total) / (pw * pc)), 0)

# 3. compress with the SVD: keep 10 dimensions, normalise for cosine similarity
U, S, _ = np.linalg.svd(ppmi)
E = U[:, :10] * S[:10]
E /= np.linalg.norm(E, axis=1, keepdims=True)

def nearest(word, k=3):
    sims = E @ E[idx[word]]
    return [vocab[i] for i in np.argsort(-sims)[1 : k + 1]]

print(nearest("cat"))      # ['mouse', 'dog', 'bone']
print(nearest("mat"))      # ['slept', 'rug', 'sat']
print(nearest("france"))   # ['germany', '.', 'of']: noisy, but germany is first

With 150 words of text the results are noisy, but the structure is already there: "rug" next to "mat", "dog" next to "cat", "germany" next to "france". Scale the corpus to billions of words and the geometry becomes remarkably rich.

word2vec: learning embeddings by prediction#

In 2013, word2vec learned embeddings with a tiny neural network instead of counting. The skip-gram variant takes a word and tries to predict the words around it. The prediction task is a pretext; the useful output is the learned embedding matrix, one row of, say, 300 numbers per word. Training pushes words with similar contexts to similar rows.

The famous result was that directions in this space carry meaning. Vector arithmetic such as

king⃗−man⃗+woman⃗≈queen⃗ \vec{\text{king}} - \vec{\text{man}} + \vec{\text{woman}} \approx \vec{\text{queen}} king​−man+woman≈queen​

roughly works, because the "male to female" offset is approximately consistent across word pairs. The effect is real but often overstated in popular accounts; the cleaner lesson is that semantic similarity becomes geometric closeness, which is what every embedding-based system relies on.

Embeddings in PyTorch#

An embedding layer is just a lookup table: a matrix of shape (vocab_size, dim) whose rows are trained by backprop along with the rest of the model. Indexing it with token ids is equivalent to multiplying one-hot vectors by the matrix, without wasting work on the zeros.

import torch, torch.nn as nn

emb = nn.Embedding(num_embeddings=50_000, embedding_dim=256)
token_ids = torch.tensor([[12, 873, 4, 99], [5, 5, 31, 0]])   # (batch=2, seq_len=4)
vectors = emb(token_ids)                                       # (2, 4, 256)

Every LLM starts with exactly this: token ids in, one learned vector per token out. In lesson 12 you will use embeddings for whole sentences and documents, which is the foundation of semantic search and RAG.

Static versus contextual embeddings

word2vec gives "bank" one vector, whether it is a river bank or a savings bank. Transformers produce contextual embeddings: the vector for "bank" changes depending on the surrounding words. That is a large part of why they understand language so much better.

Recurrent neural networks#

Embeddings handle individual words; language is sequences. A feed-forward network takes a fixed-size input, but sentences vary in length and order matters ("dog bites man" is not "man bites dog").

A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads a sequence one step at a time, carrying a hidden state hth_tht​ that summarises everything so far:

ht=tanh⁡(xtWx+ht−1Wh+b) h_t = \tanh(x_t W_x + h_{t-1} W_h + b) ht​=tanh(xt​Wx​+ht−1​Wh​+b)

The same weights are used at every step, so the network handles any length.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
d_in, d_h = 8, 16
Wx = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (d_in, d_h))
Wh = rng.normal(0, 0.3, (d_h, d_h))
b = np.zeros(d_h)

xs = rng.normal(size=(5, d_in))        # a sequence of 5 input vectors (e.g. word embeddings)
h = np.zeros(d_h)                      # start with an empty memory
for x in xs:
    h = np.tanh(x @ Wx + h @ Wh + b)   # update the memory with each new input
print(h.shape)                         # (16,): a summary of the whole sequence

Why plain RNNs forget#

Training an RNN backpropagates through every time step, multiplying by WhW_hWh​ (and the tanh derivative) again and again. Repeated multiplication either shrinks or explodes:

for factor in [0.9, 1.0, 1.1]:
    print(factor, factor ** 50)       # 0.0052, 1.0, 117.4

After 50 steps, a factor of 0.9 leaves half a percent of the gradient: the network effectively cannot learn that a word at the start of a paragraph matters for one at the end. This is the vanishing gradient problem in time.

LSTMs and GRUs#

The LSTM (long short-term memory) adds a separate cell state that flows through time with only gentle, gated modifications, and three learned gates decide what to forget, what to write and what to output. Because the cell state is updated additively, gradients can flow across many more steps (the same insight as residual connections). The GRU is a simpler variant with two gates. From roughly 2014 to 2017, LSTMs were the state of the art for translation, speech recognition and language modelling.

lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=256, hidden_size=512, num_layers=2, batch_first=True)
out, (h_n, c_n) = lstm(vectors)        # vectors: (2, 4, 256) from the embedding above
print(out.shape)                       # (2, 4, 512): a hidden state for every position

Sequence-to-sequence and the bottleneck#

For translation, an encoder RNN reads the source sentence into a final hidden state, and a decoder RNN generates the target sentence word by word from it. This seq2seq design (2014) worked, but with a glaring problem: the entire meaning of the source sentence, whether five words or fifty, has to squeeze through one fixed-size vector. Long sentences degraded badly.

The fix that changed everything: attention#

In 2014, Bahdanau and colleagues let the decoder look back at every encoder hidden state at each output step, computing a weighted average where the weights say which source words are relevant right now. Translating "chat", the decoder attends mostly to "cat". The bottleneck vanished, and long-sentence translation improved dramatically.

Then, in 2017, "Attention Is All You Need" asked: if attention is doing the important work, why keep the recurrence at all? Recurrence forces you to process tokens one after another, which is slow and makes long-range connections hard. The transformer drops it entirely and lets every token attend to every other token directly, in parallel. That is the subject of the next lesson.

  1. Find the nearest neighbours of "python", "spectrometer" and "transformer". What do the results tell you about the training corpus?
  2. Test analogies with most_similar(positive=["king", "woman"], negative=["man"]), plus three of your own. How many work?
  3. Compute cosine similarity between "doctor" and "nurse", and between "doctor" with "man" and "woman". What does this suggest about bias in data-driven representations?
Show what to look for 

import gensim.downloader as api
wv = api.load("glove-wiki-gigaword-50")
for w in ["python", "spectrometer", "transformer"]:
    print(w, [x for x, _ in wv.most_similar(w, topn=5)])
print(wv.most_similar(positive=["king", "woman"], negative=["man"], topn=3))
print(wv.similarity("doctor", "man"), wv.similarity("doctor", "woman"))
1. "python" sits near other snakes as well as programming terms, because Wikipedia and news text from that era use both senses. "transformer" sits near electrical and toy-franchise words, not neural networks: these vectors predate the 2017 paper. Embeddings reflect their corpus and its date. 2. "queen" typically comes out on top for the classic analogy, but many hand-made analogies fail or return near-synonyms of the input words. The geometry is suggestive, not a reliable reasoning engine. 3. You will usually see measurable gender asymmetries for occupation words. Models absorb the statistical patterns of their training text, including social biases. Lesson 18 returns to how to measure and mitigate this.

Check yourself#

1. What is the main weakness of one-hot word vectors?

All one-hot vectors are orthogonal: the dot product between any two different words is 0.

2. What idea underlies both count-based embeddings and word2vec?

The distributional hypothesis: you know a word by the company it keeps.

3. An nn.Embedding layer with 50,000 entries and dimension 256 receives token ids of shape (8, 128). What is the output shape?

Each id is replaced by its 256-dimensional row.

4. Why do plain RNNs struggle with long-range dependencies?

LSTMs ease this with additive cell-state updates; transformers avoid it by connecting every token directly.

5. What problem did attention originally solve in sequence-to-sequence models?

The decoder could look back at every encoder state and focus on the relevant ones.

Key takeaways

  • Models need numbers; one-hot vectors carry no meaning. Embeddings place similar words close together.
  • The distributional hypothesis (context defines meaning) underlies count-based vectors, word2vec and, ultimately, LLMs.
  • An embedding layer is a trainable lookup table. Transformers make embeddings contextual.
  • RNNs read sequences step by step with a hidden state, but suffer vanishing gradients through time; LSTMs mitigate this.
  • Seq2seq models squeezed meaning through one vector. Attention removed the bottleneck, and the transformer kept only attention.
